01 Why do activations have gradients if only weights & biases get updated?

?
Your Confusion

I thought only weights and biases get changed. So why does \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a \) even exist? What is the gradient of an activation for?

✓
The Answer

You are completely correct — only \( w \) and \( b \) are ever updated. Activations are computed during the forward pass and then fixed. They are never touched by gradient descent.

But to compute \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w \) — which IS what gradient descent needs — the chain rule forces us to pass through \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a \) first, because the activation sits between the weight and the loss in the dependency chain. \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a \) is a mathematical stepping stone, not a target.

⬡
Analogy — The Blame Chain

A factory: Machine settings → Worker output → Final product. The product is defective. You want to adjust the machine settings (= weights). You can't reach the machine directly — you first measure how much the worker's output (= activation) contributed to the defect. That intermediate blame score is \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a \). It is a relay baton, not a destination. You never fix the activation directly. You fix the weights that produced it.

Gradient Computation

  • Computes \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a \) at every activation
  • Computes \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z \) at every pre-activation
  • Computes \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w \) and \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial b \)
  • Nothing is changed at this stage — just measuring slopes

Parameter Update

  • Updates only \( w \) and \( b \)
  • Uses the gradients from the left column
  • Rule: \( w \leftarrow w - \alpha\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w} \)
  • Activations are never touched — they improve naturally on the next forward pass

✓ Gets Updated — Learnable Parameters

  • \( w^{[l]}_{ji} \) — all weights
  • \( b^{[l]}_j \) — all biases

Never Updated — Computed Values

  • \( z^{[l]}_j \) — pre-activations
  • \( a^{[l]}_j \) — activations
  • Their gradients \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z \), \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a \) are relay batons only

02 Strict, Consistent Naming Convention

!
The Problem

The original file mixed a₁, h₁, z_out, ŷ, and flat-numbered w₁…w₆ with no layer index. One rule is enforced below, everywhere, without exception.

\( w^{[l]}_{ji} \) w, layer l, to-j from-i Weight in layer \(l\) connecting neuron \(i\) (previous layer) → neuron \(j\) (this layer). Destination subscript first. Updated

\( a^{[l]}_j \) a, layer l, neuron j Post-activation. After applying \(\sigma\): \( a^{[l]}_j = \sigma(z^{[l]}_j) \). This is what leaves the neuron and travels forward. Computed
\( a^{[L]}_1 = \hat{y} \) final-layer activation The network's prediction. Same symbol system — just the last layer's activation. No special symbol needed. Computed
\( \mathcal{L} \) script-L, "the loss" A single scalar measuring how wrong the prediction is. All of backprop computes gradients of \(\mathcal{L}\). Minimise this
\( \dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{[l]}_j} \) gradient of loss w.r.t. activation The relay baton. Computed en-route backwards. Used only to find the next gradient further back. Never used to update anything directly. Messenger

04 Forward Pass — Real Numbers, Strict Names

Setup: \( x_1 = 0.5 \), \( x_2 = 0.8 \), \( y = 1 \), all weights \( = 0.5 \), all biases \( = 0 \), activation = sigmoid \( \sigma \).

F1
Pre-activations of hidden layer — \( z^{[1]}_1 \) and \( z^{[1]}_2 \)
\[ z^{[1]}_1 = w^{[1]}_{11}\,x_1 + w^{[1]}_{12}\,x_2 + b^{[1]}_1 = 0.5(0.5) + 0.5(0.8) + 0 = 0.65 \] \[ z^{[1]}_2 = w^{[1]}_{21}\,x_1 + w^{[1]}_{22}\,x_2 + b^{[1]}_2 = 0.65 \]

\( z \) is just a weighted sum. The neuron has received its inputs but has not yet "fired" through the activation function.

F2
Apply \( \sigma \) to get post-activations — \( a^{[1]}_1 \) and \( a^{[1]}_2 \)
\[ a^{[1]}_1 = \sigma(z^{[1]}_1) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-0.65}} \approx 0.657 \qquad a^{[1]}_2 \approx 0.657 \]

\( a^{[1]}_1 \) reads as "activation of neuron 1 in layer 1". This is what leaves the neuron and travels to the next layer.

F3
Pre-activation of output neuron — \( z^{[2]} \)
\[ z^{[2]} = w^{[2]}_{11}\,a^{[1]}_1 + w^{[2]}_{12}\,a^{[1]}_2 + b^{[2]} = 0.5(0.657) + 0.5(0.657) + 0 = 0.657 \]
F4
Output prediction — \( a^{[2]}_1 = \hat{y} \)
\[ a^{[2]}_1 = \hat{y} = \sigma(z^{[2]}) = \sigma(0.657) \approx 0.659 \]
F5
Compute loss \( \mathcal{L} \)
\[ \mathcal{L} = (\hat{y} - y)^2 = (0.659 - 1)^2 = (-0.341)^2 \approx 0.116 \]

Forward pass complete. Now backprop figures out exactly how much each weight contributed to this 0.116.

05 Backward Pass — Gradient Chain, Step by Step

We work right to left. Activation and pre-activation gradients are computed as intermediate steps — they update nothing. Weight gradients are what gradient descent actually consumes.

B1 — Gradient w.r.t. the output activation

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{[2]}_1} = 2(\hat{y} - y) = 2(0.659 - 1) = -0.682 \]
∂L/∂a²₁ = −0.682 Messenger entering from the loss. a²₁ is not updated. This value carries forward into B2.

B2 — Differentiate through the output activation function

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{[2]}} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{[2]}_1} \cdot \sigma'(z^{[2]}) = -0.682 \times \underbrace{0.659(1-0.659)}_{\sigma' = 0.225} \approx -0.153 \]
∂L/∂z² = −0.153 Still a messenger. z² is not updated either. Carries forward into B3 and B4.

B3 — Gradient w.r.t. output weights — these get updated

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{[2]}_{11}} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{[2]}} \cdot a^{[1]}_1 = -0.153 \times 0.657 \approx -0.101 \] \[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{[2]}_{12}} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{[2]}} \cdot a^{[1]}_2 \approx -0.101 \]
Gradient Descent Update — \( \alpha = 0.1 \)
w²₁₁ ← w²₁₁ − α · (∂L/∂w²₁₁) = 0.5 − 0.1(−0.101) = 0.5101 Weight updated. Positive step since gradient was negative (we were under-predicting).
w²₁₂ ← w²₁₂ − α · (∂L/∂w²₁₂) = 0.5101 Same update by symmetry.

B4 — Propagate gradient back to hidden activations

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{[1]}_1} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{[2]}} \cdot w^{[2]}_{11} = -0.153 \times 0.5 = -0.077 \] \[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{[1]}_2} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{[2]}} \cdot w^{[2]}_{12} = -0.077 \]
⚠️
This is exactly where your confusion was

We just computed \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^{[1]}_1 = -0.077 \). We will never use this to update \( a^{[1]}_1 \). We computed it because the next step needs it to reach \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z^{[1]}_1 \), which then reaches \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w^{[1]}_{11} \) — the weight gradient we actually care about. The activation gradient exists for one reason only: it is a required intermediate in the chain rule.

B5 — Through the hidden activation function

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{[1]}_1} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{[1]}_1} \cdot \sigma'(z^{[1]}_1) = -0.077 \times 0.657(1-0.657) = -0.077 \times 0.225 \approx -0.0174 \] \[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{[1]}_2} \approx -0.0174 \quad \text{(symmetric)} \]

B6 — Gradient w.r.t. input weights — these get updated

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{[1]}_{11}} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{[1]}_1} \cdot x_1 = -0.0174 \times 0.5 = -0.0087 \] \[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{[1]}_{12}} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{[1]}_1} \cdot x_2 = -0.0174 \times 0.8 = -0.0139 \]
Gradient Descent Update — \( \alpha = 0.1 \)
w¹₁₁ ← 0.5 − 0.1(−0.0087) = 0.5009 Tiny step — this weight is further from the loss, so less blame reaches it.
w¹₁₂ ← 0.5 − 0.1(−0.0139) = 0.5014 Slightly larger step because x₂ = 0.8 > x₁ = 0.5.

07 The One Rule to Internalise

◈
The Golden Rule of Backprop

Backprop computes \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial(\text{everything}) \) — weights, biases, activations, pre-activations — but only \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w \) and \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial b \) are used to change anything. All other gradients are mathematically required relay points. They carry blame backwards, neuron by neuron, until that blame reaches a learnable parameter — and only then is something updated.

Why does PyTorch store .grad for activations at all?

When you call loss.backward(), PyTorch accumulates \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\,\text{tensor} \) for every tensor with requires_grad=True. For parameters (weights, biases), the optimizer reads .grad and updates them. For intermediate activations, PyTorch discards their gradients after the backward pass by default — unless you explicitly call tensor.retain_grad(). This is the library itself confirming the rule: activation gradients are temporary messengers.

✓
Intuition Check

We found \( \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^{[1]}_1 = -0.077 \). This means: if \( a^{[1]}_1 \) increased by 1, the loss would decrease by 0.077. Can you directly increase \( a^{[1]}_1 \)? No — it is just \( \sigma(z^{[1]}_1) \), a computed output. What you can do is increase \( w^{[1]}_{11} \) and \( w^{[1]}_{12} \), which controls \( z^{[1]}_1 \), which naturally pushes \( a^{[1]}_1 \) higher on the next forward pass. That is exactly what backprop does.
